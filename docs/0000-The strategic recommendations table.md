The strategic recommendations table is the **synthesis layer**: it takes the six architectural layers and asks not *what* they should do but *when* they should do it, in what order, and with what dependencies. The grid format is useful for orientation, but it hides three things that determine whether the roadmap succeeds: **cross-layer dependencies** (you cannot build an agentic prover before the DSL exists), **acceptance criteria** (what "done" means for each milestone), and **the critical path** (which sequence of milestones gates everything else). Below is the expanded roadmap, organized as three waves rather than three time windows, because the waves are gated by *capabilities*, not by calendar dates.

---

## The Case for Wave-Based Phasing

The short/medium/long framing is familiar, but it can mislead: it suggests that the three horizons are independent tracks that run in parallel at different speeds. In practice, they are **gated**. You cannot integrate property-based testing (medium-term verification) before the core modules are `sorry`-free (short-term verification), because property tests cannot be attached to a module whose type signatures are still changing. You cannot adopt an agentic prover (medium-term tooling) before the skeleton registry exists (short-term substrate), because the prover's work queue *is* the skeleton registry. The roadmap is a **dependency DAG**, and the wave structure makes the gates explicit.

The three waves are:

- **Wave 1 (1–3 months): Make the trust surface visible.** The goal is not to improve the system but to make its current trust posture *observable*. Axiom audits, `sorry`-free CI gates, proof-health reports, and a documented façade are all "measurement" milestones: they do not change the formalization; they reveal what it currently assumes.
- **Wave 2 (3–9 months): Make the trust surface productive.** The goal is to make the formalization *grow faster than it accumulates debt*. The DSL, the property tests, the differential tests, the provenance hashing, and the runtime monitors all contribute to this: they increase the rate at which legal theorems can be formalized while simultaneously constraining the ways in which the formalization can drift.
- **Wave 3 (9–18 months): Make the trust surface consumable.** The goal is to make the formalization *usable by parties who do not trust the producers*. The certificate registry, the WASM build, the replay façade, and the security audit module all serve this goal: they allow a downstream consumer — a regulator, a court, a compliance checker — to verify LexLean's claims without trusting LexLean's infrastructure.

## Wave 1 (1–3 months): Make the Trust Surface Visible

### Substrate: Audit the Axiom Surface

**Deliverable.** A machine-readable `axiom-ledger.json` that maps every public declaration in LexLean to its transitive axiom closure, plus a `permitted-axioms.json` that declares the project's trust policy.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- Every public declaration has an entry in the ledger.
- The ledger is generated by a script (not by hand) and is regenerable from the pinned commit.
- The ledger distinguishes between kernel axioms (`propext`, `Classical.choice`, `Quot.sound`), Mathlib axioms, LexLean-specific axioms, and `sorryAx`.
- The ledger is a CI artifact: every PR that changes a declaration's axiom profile produces a diff.

**Dependencies.** None. This is the first milestone because everything else depends on knowing the current trust surface.

**Effort.** Low. The `unsorry` project's `AxiomAudit/Main.lean` is a working template; adapting it to LexLean is a matter of enumerating the public modules and running `Lean.collectAxioms`.

**Risk.** The audit may reveal that LexLean currently depends on undeclared axioms. If so, the audit is not a failure — it is the *point*. The short-term response is to document the axioms; the medium-term response (Wave 2) is to eliminate them.

### Verification: Add `sorry`-Free CI Gate and Proof-Health Report

**Deliverable.** A CI job that fails on any `sorry` in a core module, plus a `proof-health.json` artifact that reports theorem count, axiom count, `sorry` count, and skeleton-registry coverage.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- The CI job runs on every PR and blocks merges that introduce `sorry` into a core module.
- The proof-health report is generated from the skeleton registry (see Substrate Wave 1) and the axiom ledger.
- The report is stable across runs: two consecutive runs on the same commit produce identical output.
- The report is posted as a PR comment so reviewers see the current proof-health posture at a glance.

**Dependencies.** Requires the axiom ledger (to compute axiom count) and the skeleton registry (to compute coverage).

**Effort.** Low to medium. The `unsorry` ADR-006 describes the layered gate pattern; the Ripple playbook describes the cold-build and `#print axioms` checks.

**Risk.** Textual grep for `sorry` is a coarse upper bound; the authoritative signal is `#print axioms`. The CI gate must use the axiom audit, not grep, as its primary check.

### Substrate: Add a Skeleton Registry

**Deliverable.** A Lean-encoded `SkeletonRegistry.lean` that enumerates every target theorem, its module, its statement, its verification status, and its dependencies.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- Every `proven` entry has a corresponding declaration whose axiom profile is compliant.
- Every `skeleton` entry has a proof body that is exactly `sorry`.
- The dependency graph is acyclic and is machine-checkable.
- The registry is imported by the proof-health report generator.

**Dependencies.** This is a Wave 1 milestone *and* a dependency for Wave 2's agentic prover. It is the single most important Wave 1 deliverable because it defines the work queue for everything that follows.

**Effort.** Medium. The registry itself is straightforward; the challenge is enumerating the target theorems, which requires input from legal experts.

**Risk.** The registry may reveal that the target theorem set is much larger than anticipated. This is a feature, not a bug: it makes the scope visible before Wave 2 commits to scaling up.

### Integration: Document a Façade API

**Deliverable.** A `LexLean.lean` façade module that re-exports the substrate types, the DSL surface (once it exists), the verification API, and the governance predicates, plus a CI check that verifies every public item is reachable through the façade.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- Every public item is reachable through `import LexLean`.
- The CI check (`lexlean-façade-check`) fails on a broken link.
- The façade structure is documented as an ADR (analogous to Prism's ADR-031).

**Dependencies.** The façade depends on the substrate types being stable. If the legal core is still being designed (Wave 2), the façade will need to be updated as the core evolves.

**Effort.** Low. The Prism façade is the template; the challenge is enforcing the reachability check in CI.

**Risk.** A façade defined too early may constrain the legal core's design. The mitigation is to define the façade at the *type* level (what the core exposes) rather than the *module* level (where it lives).

### Observability: Add Proof-Search Telemetry

**Deliverable.** A telemetry hook that records tactic-level timing and outcome for every proof attempt, plus a `telemetry.jsonl` artifact that accumulates records across commits.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- Every proof attempt emits a record with theorem name, tactic sequence, per-tactic timing, outcome, and axiom profile.
- The records are aggregated into a per-commit summary that flags performance regressions (proofs that are >2× slower than their historical median).
- The telemetry hook is opt-in via a `set_option` so it does not slow down normal builds.

**Dependencies.** Requires the axiom audit (to record axiom profiles) and the skeleton registry (to record registry entries).

**Effort.** Low to medium. Lean's `trace.profiler` provides the timing data; the challenge is parsing the trace tree into structured events.

**Risk.** Telemetry can itself be slow. The mitigation is to run telemetry only on a nightly job or on a designated "telemetry" CI lane, not on every PR.

## Wave 2 (3–9 months): Make the Trust Surface Productive

### Substrate: Formalize Legal Semantics as Inductive Types

**Deliverable.** A `LexLean.Core` module that defines `LegalSource`, `Norm`, `Derivation`, and `Certificate` as inductive types, replacing any axiomatic legal primitives.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- Every legal primitive is defined inductively; no `axiom` declarations in the core.
- The inductive definitions are kernel-checked and strictly positive.
- The core's axiom profile is exactly `{propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound}`.
- The inductive definitions are documented with backlinks to their legal sources.

**Dependencies.** Requires the axiom audit (Wave 1) to identify which primitives are currently axiomatic, and legal-expert input to design the inductive structure.

**Effort.** High. This is the intellectual core of LexLean. The inductive design must capture the structure of legal reasoning without over-committing to a particular jurisprudential theory.

**Risk.** The inductive definitions may be too restrictive (excluding valid legal reasoning) or too permissive (admitting invalid reasoning). The mitigation is to validate the definitions against a corpus of test cases (see Verification: differential tests).

### Tooling: Build a Legal DSL Macro

**Deliverable.** A `legal_rule` syntax category and elaborator that translates surface legal syntax into the inductive core types, with bridge theorems for each construct.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- A legal expert can write a rule in surface syntax and see it elaborated to a core term.
- Every DSL construct has a bridge theorem stating that the elaborated term is semantically equivalent to the surface form.
- The elaborator is hygienic and its output is deterministic.
- Round-trip fuzzing passes on a corpus of surface expressions.

**Dependencies.** Requires the inductive core (Substrate Wave 2). The DSL cannot elaborate to types that do not yet exist.

**Effort.** High. The `verity_contract` macro is the template, but the legal DSL is more complex because it must handle defeasibility, temporal qualifiers, and cross-references.

**Risk.** A DSL that is too expressive becomes a programming language; a DSL that is too restrictive cannot express real legal rules. The mitigation is to start with a core fragment (conditions, obligations, permissions) and extend incrementally.

### Verification: Integrate Property-Based Testing and Differential Tests

**Deliverable.** A `LexLean.Tests` module with Plausible properties for executable fragments, plus a differential-testing harness that compares the Lean model against a reference implementation.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- Every executable fragment has at least one property-based test.
- The differential-testing harness generates random legal scenarios, runs both implementations, and compares conclusions and derivation trees.
- Divergences are shrunk to minimal repros and reported as CI failures.

**Dependencies.** Requires the inductive core (for the oracle) and the DSL (for generating random scenarios).

**Effort.** Medium to high. The property tests are straightforward; the differential-testing harness requires a reference implementation, which may not exist yet.

**Risk.** Differential testing requires two implementations. If the reference implementation is itself derived from the Lean model, the tests catch translation bugs but not specification bugs. The mitigation is to use a *hand-written* reference implementation for a subset of the derivation rules.

### Governance: Add Provenance Hashing

**Deliverable.** A `provenance-ledger.json` that records the content hash of every source file, `.olean` file, axiom audit, and certificate.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- Every artifact is identified by its SHA-256 hash.
- The ledger is append-only and tamper-evident.
- The ledger is published as a CI artifact and can be independently re-computed.
- Each theorem's registry entry references its ledger entry.

**Dependencies.** Requires the axiom audit and skeleton registry (Wave 1), and the DSL and inductive core (Wave 2) to have stable artifacts worth hashing.

**Effort.** Low to medium. The content-addressing is straightforward; the challenge is integrating it with the CI pipeline so that every commit produces a consistent ledger.

**Risk.** The ledger may grow large. The mitigation is to store only the *hash chain* in the repository and publish the full artifacts to a content-addressed store (e.g., IPFS or a Zenodo deposit).

### Integration: Build WASM / JSON-RPC Interface

**Deliverable.** A WASM build of the executable fragments and a JSON-RPC service that exposes the certificate checker and the axiom auditor.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- The WASM module runs in a browser without the Lean toolchain.
- The JSON-RPC service accepts a certificate and returns a proof-carrying response.
- The client can replay the proof against a pinned environment.
- The WASM module's axiom profile matches the Lean source's axiom profile.

**Dependencies.** Requires the inductive core (for the certificate format) and the provenance ledger (for the content hashes).

**Effort.** Medium to high. The Lean-Rust architecture is the template, but adapting it to LexLean's specific fragments requires care.

**Risk.** The FFI boundary is a potential source of unsoundness. The mitigation is to keep the exported API narrow and to boundary-verify the serialization schema.

### Observability: Runtime Invariant Monitors

**Deliverable.** A `LexLean.Monitor` module with Lean-checkable predicates for certificate well-formedness, citation freshness, axiom compliance, and resource bounds, compiled into the WASM/JSON-RPC artifacts.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- Every monitor predicate has a soundness theorem (`MonitorInvariant cert ctx = true → ValidCertificate cert ctx`).
- The monitor runs in the extracted code and rejects invalid certificates before they reach the kernel.
- Every monitor decision emits a provenance chain entry.

**Dependencies.** Requires the WASM/JSON-RPC interface (Integration Wave 2) and the provenance ledger (Governance Wave 2).

**Effort.** Medium. The SC-NeuroCore hardware monitor is the template; the challenge is ensuring that the monitor's logic is extracted correctly.

**Risk.** A runtime monitor that is too strict rejects valid certificates; one that is too permissive admits invalid ones. The mitigation is to prove soundness (the monitor never accepts invalid certificates) and to test completeness empirically.

## Wave 3 (9–18 months): Make the Trust Surface Consumable

### Substrate: Achieve Zero-Axiom Core

**Deliverable.** A `LexLean.Core` module whose axiom profile is exactly `{propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound}` — no custom axioms, no `sorry`, no `native_decide` in core modules.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- `#print axioms` on every core declaration returns a subset of the kernel axioms.
- The skeleton registry's core entries all have status `proven`.
- The proof-health report shows zero custom axioms and zero `sorry` in core.

**Dependencies.** Requires the inductive core (Wave 2) and the DSL (Wave 2) to have eliminated the need for axiomatic legal primitives.

**Effort.** High. Eliminating the last axioms is the hardest part of any formalization.

**Risk.** Some legal primitives may resist inductive formalization. The mitigation is to isolate them in a clearly named module with a documented trust level, rather than admitting them into the core.

### Verification: Full Dual-Assurance Pipeline

**Deliverable.** A CI pipeline that runs property-based tests, differential tests, macro fuzzing, cold builds, axiom audits, and proof-health reports on every commit, with all gates enforced.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- Every gate from the verification pipeline layer is active.
- Every gate has a documented failure mode and a documented remediation path.
- The pipeline produces a single, machine-readable verification report per commit.

**Dependencies.** Requires all Wave 2 verification milestones.

**Effort.** Medium. The pipeline is an integration of existing components; the challenge is ensuring that the components compose correctly.

**Risk.** A pipeline with too many gates becomes a bottleneck; one with too few gates admits drift. The mitigation is to distinguish between *blocking* gates (axiom audit, `sorry`-free) and *advisory* gates (telemetry, coverage).

### Tooling: Adopt Agentic Prover (LEAP-Style)

**Deliverable.** A blueprint-then-proof workflow where an LLM-in-Lean agent generates a DAG of sub-lemmas, dispatches them to prover agents, and repairs failures via compiler feedback.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- The agent can close a target theorem from the skeleton registry without human intervention.
- The agent's proofs pass the axiom audit and the `sorry`-free gate.
- The agent updates the skeleton registry when it completes a proof.

**Dependencies.** Requires the skeleton registry (Wave 1), the DSL (Wave 2), and the verification pipeline (Wave 3).

**Effort.** High. The LEAP framework is the template, but adapting it to legal reasoning requires domain-specific retrieval and repair heuristics.

**Risk.** The agent may produce proofs that are technically correct but legally meaningless. The mitigation is the citation layer: every theorem must link to a legal source, and the agent must not fabricate citations.

### Governance: Publish Certificate Registry

**Deliverable.** A DOI-frozen `CertificateRegistry` that lists all externally verifiable claims, their Lean modules, their axiom profiles, their legal citations, and their provenance chain entries.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- Every certified claim has a valid legal citation with a current `text_hash`.
- Every certified claim's axiom profile is compliant.
- The registry is reproducible: an independent verifier can rebuild the proofs and confirm the registry.
- The registry has a DOI and is cited in the project's documentation.

**Dependencies.** Requires the provenance ledger (Wave 2), the zero-axiom core (Wave 3), and the full verification pipeline (Wave 3).

**Effort.** Medium. The ZLT registry is the template; the challenge is ensuring that every claim meets the certification threshold.

**Risk.** A registry that includes claims whose citations become stale is worse than no registry. The mitigation is the stale-citation gate (Wave 2/3).

### Integration: Replay Façade for Independent Verifiers

**Deliverable.** A `lexlean-verify` executable that accepts a compact certificate and performs the three-phase replay (deserialize, spec-replay, axiom audit).

**Acceptance criteria.**
- The façade runs in a minimal environment (no Lean toolchain required for the client; only for the verifier).
- The façade's output is deterministic and machine-readable.
- The façade is independently re-runnable: any third party can download the certificate and the pinned environment and reproduce the verdict.

**Dependencies.** Requires the WASM/JSON-RPC interface (Wave 2), the certificate registry (Wave 3), and the runtime monitors (Wave 2).

**Effort.** Medium. The `prism_verify` crate is the template.

**Risk.** A replay façade that is too complex to run is not a replay façade. The mitigation is to keep the façade minimal: it should depend on the Lean kernel and nothing else.

### Observability: Security Audit Module

**Deliverable.** A `LexLean.Audit` module that performs per-declaration axiom footprint audits, trust-policy compliance checks, `sorry` detection, and drift detection, and emits a replayable audit receipt.

**Acceptance criteria.**
- The audit is reproducible: `lake env lean --run LexLean.Audit.Replay` produces the same receipt on any machine.
- The audit distinguishes between trust levels (core, computation, skeleton, experimental).
- The audit's output is appended to the provenance chain.

**Dependencies.** Requires the certificate registry (Wave 3) and the provenance ledger (Wave 2).

**Effort.** Medium. The `unsorry` `AxiomAudit/Main.lean` is the template.

**Risk.** The audit may produce false positives (flagging permitted axioms as violations). The mitigation is to keep the trust policy small and explicit.

## Cross-Layer Dependencies: The Critical Path

The roadmap is not a grid; it is a DAG. The critical path — the sequence of milestones that gates everything else — is:

```
Axiom Audit (Substrate W1)
    ↓
Skeleton Registry (Substrate W1)
    ↓
Inductive Core (Substrate W2)
    ↓
Legal DSL (Tooling W2)
    ↓
Agentic Prover (Tooling W3)
    ↓
Certificate Registry (Governance W3)
    ↓
Replay Façade (Integration W3)
```

The **skeleton registry** is the single most important Wave 1 deliverable because it defines the work queue for the agentic prover, the coverage metric for the proof-health report, and the claim list for the certificate registry. If the skeleton registry is wrong — if it omits target theorems or misstates their dependencies — everything downstream inherits the error.

The **inductive core** is the single most important Wave 2 deliverable because it defines the types that the DSL elaborates to, the certificates that the WASM build serializes, and the theorems that the certificate registry certifies. If the inductive core is wrong — if it fails to capture the structure of legal reasoning — everything downstream inherits the error.

The **legal DSL** is the single most important Wave 2 tooling deliverable because it determines the rate at which legal theorems can be formalized. Without the DSL, every theorem requires a Lean expert; with the DSL, a legal expert can draft rules that a Lean expert reviews. This is the difference between a formalization that grows linearly with the number of Lean experts and one that grows linearly with the number of legal experts.

## Risk Register

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Axiom audit reveals widespread undeclared axioms** | High | Medium | Document in Wave 1; eliminate in Wave 2; isolate in a trust-level module if elimination is infeasible |
| **Inductive core is too restrictive** | Medium | High | Validate against a corpus of test cases; extend incrementally; keep the core minimal and push domain-specific rules to extensions |
| **DSL is too expressive** | Medium | Medium | Start with a core fragment; add constructs only when they are needed by real legal rules |
| **Agentic prover produces vacuous proofs** | Medium | High | Require every theorem to have a legal citation; require the citation's `text_hash` to be current; audit non-vacuity via concrete witnesses |
| **Certificate registry includes stale citations** | High | High | The stale-citation gate: every CI run re-resolves URIs and re-computes `text_hash`; a stale citation fails the build |
| **Replay façade diverges from the producer** | Medium | High | Pin the Lean toolchain and the LexLean commit; publish the environment hash alongside every certificate; test the façade against a reference producer in CI |
| **Telemetry slows down the build** | High | Low | Run telemetry on a nightly lane, not on every PR; make it opt-in via `set_option` |

## The "Day 1" Checklist

If you want to start tomorrow, the minimum viable Wave 1 is:

1. **Enumerate the public modules.** List every module in LexLean and every public declaration it exposes.
2. **Run the axiom audit.** For each public declaration, run `#print axioms` and record the output. This is the axiom ledger.
3. **Identify the `sorry` locations.** Grep for `sorry` and record the file, line, and surrounding theorem. This is the initial skeleton registry (in prose form).
4. **Write the façade module.** Create `LexLean.lean` that imports every public module and re-exports its public items.
5. **Write the CI job.** A single GitHub Actions workflow that runs `lake build`, the axiom audit, and the façade reachability check.
6. **Publish the artifacts.** Commit the axiom ledger, the skeleton registry (in prose), and the CI workflow to the repository.

This is achievable in a week by one person. It produces no new theorems, no new proofs, and no new formalization. What it produces is **visibility**: the project's current trust surface is now machine-readable, and every subsequent commit is measured against it.

## Refining the Roadmap

The roadmap above is *domain-general*: it assumes LexLean is a Lean 4 formalization of legal reasoning, but it does not assume any particular legal domain, any particular set of statutes, or any particular jurisprudential theory. To refine it into a concrete, prioritized plan, I would need to know:

- **What LexLean currently contains.** A file listing (even just the top-level modules) would let me identify which Wave 1 milestones are already partially complete and which Wave 2 milestones are gated by missing substrate.
- **The intended downstream consumers.** If LexLean is intended for regulator dashboards, the WASM build and the replay façade are higher priority than the agentic prover. If it is intended for internal research, the DSL and the inductive core are higher priority.
- **The legal domain.** Contract law, statutory interpretation, and constitutional law have different formalization requirements. A DSL for contract conditions is different from a DSL for constitutional balancing tests.
- **The team's composition.** If the team has Lean experts but no legal experts, the DSL is the highest-priority Wave 1 tooling milestone. If the team has legal experts but no Lean experts, the agentic prover and the DSL are both Wave 1 priorities.
- **The governance context.** If LexLean's proofs are intended to be cited in legal proceedings, the certificate registry and the replay façade are Wave 1 priorities, not Wave 3. If they are intended for internal use, they can wait.

Share whatever you have — a file listing, a module description, a README, even a rough sketch of the intended domain — and I can convert this roadmap into a concrete, sequenced plan with named deliverables, owners, and dates.
